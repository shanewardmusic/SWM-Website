About

Ju jutsu is built on adaptability: meeting force with timing, balance and considered technique.

At Nikido Ju Jutsu, training is an opportunity to grow in both skill and character. Students work at their own level in a structured environment, learning practical movement while developing awareness, control and respect for their training partners.

Control Move with purpose, apply technique responsibly and learn to stay composed under pressure.
Adaptability Explore balance, leverage and timing across throws, holds, locks and defensive movement.
Respect Progress through consistent practice, shared effort and care for everyone on the mat.

A living inheritance

Martial traditions do not travel through time as simple lists of names. They move through people: through instruction, practice, records, organisations, migration and the choices later teachers make about what must be preserved.

This account follows the ancestry received by Nikido Ju Jutsu from the early Yōshin traditions, through Shin no Shintō-ryū and Tenjin Shin’yō-ryū, to the Australian transmission associated with Ryūgorō Fukushima, Leonard Noyes, Wallace Brown, Bruce Watts and Alfred E. Ward. Related arts are included where they illuminate shared principles, without presenting them as direct Nikido succession.

The history draws on institutional records, academic research, recognised school histories and Nikido’s own genealogy and oral record. Secure relationships are stated directly; where evidence is less complete, the language remains deliberately measured.

The willow heart and the meaning of jū

Nikido’s deeper ancestry begins in the broad family of Yōshin-ryū traditions—the “Willow Heart School.” Its best-known origin story associates Akiyama Shirōbei Yoshitoki with the observation that rigid branches break beneath snow while the willow bends, sheds the weight and returns to form. The story describes active flexibility: yielding only as required, preserving structure and recovering control.[1][2]

The principle of the soft overcoming the hard is older than Akiyama. His importance lies in giving that principle an influential Japanese martial and therapeutic form—joining adaptability with posture, timing, striking, seizing, throwing and restoration. The pairing of dangerous knowledge with restorative practice also carried an ethical lesson: intimate knowledge of harm creates a corresponding duty of care.[3][4]

Traditions converge

In the eighteenth century, Yamamoto Minzaemon Hidehaya systematised Shin no Shintō-ryū from the Yōshin milieu. Its published genealogy continues through Doi Mutansai Yasunobu to Honma Jōemon Kanzan Masatō. In the early nineteenth century, Iso Mataemon Masatari studied a Yōshin tradition under Hitotsuyanagi Oribe Yoshimichi and Shin no Shintō-ryū under Honma, then combined and revised what he had received into Tenjin Shin’yō-ryū.[5][6][7]

Tenjin Shin’yō-ryū answered the close-range realities of urban conflict with seated and standing methods, releases, throws, locks, strangulations, strikes, vital-point study and resuscitation. Striking and grappling were not treated as separate worlds: atemi could disrupt posture or attention so that a throw, restraint or escape became possible. Technique was understood as a relationship between distance, timing, balance and intent.

Related paths, shared lessons

Later arts transformed related knowledge in different ways. Kanō Jigorō studied Tenjin Shin’yō-ryū and Kitō-ryū before founding Kōdōkan Jūdō, turning jū into a broad educational principle supported by kata, randori and mutual welfare. Shindō Yōshin-ryū joined jūjutsu with weapon-informed body method; Ōtsuka Hironori combined that inheritance with Okinawan karate in Wadō-ryū.[9][14][17]

Ueshiba Morihei’s Aikidō arose chiefly from Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu under Takeda Sōkaku, alongside a wider life of martial and spiritual study. Jūdō, Wadō-ryū and Aikidō illuminate ideas that matter to Nikido—balance, receiving force, timing, non-collision and integrated movement—but they neither descend from nor define Nikido’s direct teaching line.[21][22][23]

Jūjutsu comes to Australia

Japanese martial arts reached Australian audiences through naval visitors, travelling performers and physical-culture circles. Fukushima Ryūgorō—later known as Ray Shima—arrived in 1906 with another Japanese performer, took part in public demonstrations and became a foundational transmitter in the lineage received by Nikido. A contemporary Sydney advertisement described extensive study and teaching responsibility at a Hagiwara School in Yokohama.[25][27][28][29]

Leonard “Len” Noyes, also recorded as Sid Neil or Sid Neal, is remembered as Fukushima’s student and founded the Mercury Jiu-Jitsu Club in 1938. The received Australian line continues through Wallace C. “Wally” Brown and Bruce K. Watts of the Wind School. Public organisational histories support important parts of this story, while the specific Noyes–Brown–Watts relationship remains chiefly a Nikido lineage record.[26][33]

Alfred E. Ward and the founding of Nikido

Nikido’s genealogy identifies Alfred E. Ward as a student in the Watts line and the founder of Nikido Ju Jutsu. Ward gathered the inherited Australian jūjutsu material into a distinct named system, formed instructors and created the source of the present succession. This is where a broad international history becomes the particular history of Nikido.

The founder’s work is preserved in more than a chart. It lives in certificates, photographs, notes, grading records, repeated phrases, corrections given on the mat and the converging recollections of direct students. Those sources are essential for distinguishing Ward’s received foundation from responsible later development.[33]

The katana as a technical lens

Nikido gives the katana a high pedagogical value. Sword practice exposes errors of posture, line, distance, timing and attention because a blade does not forgive casual angle or disconnected movement. It can educate the empty hand by making centre line, ma-ai, hasuji, integrated action and zanshin unmistakably clear.

This does not claim one unchanged sword syllabus running through every generation. Rather, related uses of weapons in Japanese and Australian martial traditions place Nikido’s practice within a recognisable heritage. Modern Kendo, Iaido and Jodo articulate principles of character, precise movement, consequence and continuing awareness that help explain why the sword remains relevant without being carried for ordinary defence.[34][36][40][42][45][47]

What it means to inherit

In Nikido, jū is responsive structure—not softness in isolation. Posture remains organised without rigidity; movement joins the hands to the back, legs and centre; timing and kuzushi change the relationship before final control is attempted. Atemi, throws, locks, strangulations, groundwork, weapons awareness and ukemi belong to a continuum of judgement rather than a catalogue of disconnected techniques.[3][11][26]

Lineage is therefore not prestige but responsibility. A living curriculum must preserve evidence, teach dangerous knowledge with care, progress from form toward appropriate resistance and adapt honestly to contemporary law, safeguarding, first aid, inclusivity and the changed risks of modern life. Custodianship means receiving, understanding, protecting and transmitting—while remaining clear about what has been inherited and what has developed since.[33]

The willow survives because it yields. The tradition survives because it remembers what must not be surrendered.
